
# Build the World's Best Native Android Photo Comparison Studio

## Master Product Vision, Research, UX/UI, Computer Vision, Architecture & Implementation Brief

**Project Type:** Native Android Application  
**Primary Language:** Kotlin  
**UI Framework:** Jetpack Compose  
**Design System:** Material Design 3 (Material 3 Expressive principles where appropriate)  
**Research Baseline:** November 13, 2026  
**Product Horizon:** 2027–2030 and beyond  
**Primary Focus:** Visual comparison, image intelligence, professional analysis, performance, configurability, and exceptional user experience.

---

# 0. YOUR MISSION

You are not being asked to create another ordinary Android application that displays two photographs next to each other.

You are being asked to research, architect, design, and implement a complete, extensible, intelligent, highly configurable visual comparison studio.

The fundamental concept is simple:

**Help people understand, inspect, compare, evaluate, organize, and make decisions about images.**

However, the ways people need to accomplish this are almost unlimited.

Someone might want to:

- Choose the sharpest photograph from a burst of 30 images.
- Compare an original photograph against its edited version.
- Compare photographs captured by two different phones or cameras.
- Understand how different camera applications process the same scene.
- Identify whether an image was compressed, resized, recolored, cropped, or manipulated.
- Compare the same location photographed months or years apart.
- Examine tiny differences between two images at individual-pixel level.
- Compare photographs with different rotations, perspectives, aspect ratios, or viewpoints.
- Determine which photograph has better sharpness, exposure, noise, or color reproduction.
- Compare multiple editing iterations.
- Examine specific regions instead of comparing entire images.
- Create visual before-and-after presentations.
- Perform professional image-quality assessments.
- Investigate inconsistencies or suspicious visual alterations.
- Compare screenshots, designs, documents, scans, illustrations, and other visual material.
- Create their own comparison workflows without waiting for us to implement every conceivable feature.

The application must be useful to ordinary people who don't understand image-processing terminology, while giving photographers, designers, developers, studio professionals, researchers, and technical investigators the controls they need.

**The central design principle: Start simple. Reveal depth progressively. Never put a ceiling on what an advanced user can accomplish.**

Do not build features merely because competing applications have them. Understand why someone needs them, what problem they solve, how they should work, and whether we can do them better.

Think independently. Challenge assumptions. Investigate alternative designs and algorithms. Discover capabilities that aren't obvious from the requirements below.

This document establishes our direction, not the boundaries of your thinking.

# 2. PRODUCT IDENTITY AND USP

We need a product that has a clear reason to exist.

The application should not be marketed as simply:

"Compare two images side by side."

That functionality already exists.

Our positioning should be closer to:

**A complete visual comparison studio that helps you discover what changed, understand why it changed, and decide what matters.**

Potential product differentiators:

### 2.1 Intelligent scene matching

Find photographs that represent the same scene, object, subject, or moment even when:

- They have different filenames.
- They are stored in different folders.
- They were captured by different cameras.
- They have different resolutions.
- They have different aspect ratios.
- They were edited differently.
- They were compressed.
- They have different orientations.
- Their timestamps are inaccurate.
- Their visual appearance has changed.

### 2.2 Intelligent comparison

Automatically suggest:

- Appropriate comparison modes.
- Alignment.
- Zoom synchronization.
- Matching regions.
- Differences worth inspecting.
- Image-quality measurements.
- Potentially better photographs.

Always let users override automatic decisions.

### 2.3 Unlimited comparison workflows

Let users create their own:

- Layouts.
- Comparison configurations.
- Analysis profiles.
- Keyboard and touch shortcuts.
- Custom metric combinations.
- Export presets.
- Workspace arrangements.
- Processing pipelines.

### 2.4 Open architecture

Treat the application as an extensible platform rather than a closed collection of screens.

Users should eventually be able to extend the application through documented configuration, custom profiles, importable definitions, and a carefully designed extension architecture.

AI agents should be able to understand the project, its configuration, its APIs, and its internal concepts.

# 5. ADVANCED IMAGE ALIGNMENT AND GEOMETRY

Two photographs of the same scene are rarely perfectly identical.

Different cameras, lenses, shooting positions, focal lengths, and editing operations introduce geometric differences.

Develop a dedicated alignment engine.

## 5.1 Manual transformations

For each image support:

- Rotation.
- Horizontal flip.
- Vertical flip.
- Translation.
- Scaling.
- Aspect-ratio adjustment.
- Crop.
- Perspective transformation.
- Fine-grained positioning.

Support touch gestures and precise numerical controls.

## 5.2 Synchronized transformations

Allow users to independently configure synchronization for:

- Zoom.
- Pan.
- Rotation.
- Center position.
- Crop.
- Viewport position.

Do not assume that synchronization should always be enabled.

Create a central synchronization controller with clearly defined behavior.

For example, when images have different aspect ratios, synchronized zoom should use a configurable reference:

- Relative zoom.
- Physical pixel scale.
- Viewport percentage.
- Reference-image scale.
- Matched-region scale.

## 5.3 Automatic alignment

Investigate and implement appropriate computer-vision approaches.

Potential algorithms:

- ORB.
- SIFT where licensing and dependency constraints permit.
- AKAZE.
- Feature matching.
- Homography estimation.
- RANSAC.
- ECC image alignment.
- Phase correlation.
- Optical flow.
- Landmark matching.

Offer:

- Translation alignment.
- Rotation and scale alignment.
- Affine alignment.
- Perspective alignment.
- Region-based alignment.

Automatically estimate alignment confidence.

Show users:

- Alignment method.
- Estimated transformation.
- Confidence.
- Number of matched features.
- Inlier ratio.
- Potential alignment failure.

Never silently apply aggressive transformations.

Preserve the original images.

Users must be able to inspect and undo every alignment operation.

## 5.4 Alignment visualization

Show optional:

- Feature points.
- Matched feature lines.
- Transformation grids.
- Overlapping boundaries.
- Cropped regions.
- Alignment error visualization.

Advanced controls should remain hidden until requested.

# 7. VISUAL INTELLIGENCE AND IMAGE ANALYTICS

Build an optional analytical layer that provides meaningful information without overwhelming ordinary users.

Every metric must have a clearly defined meaning.

## 7.1 Image similarity

Investigate:

- Exact pixel equality.
- Perceptual hashes.
- Difference ratios.
- SSIM.
- Multi-scale SSIM where appropriate.
- Feature-based similarity.
- Embedding-based similarity.
- Region similarity.
- Histogram similarity.

Do not combine unrelated metrics into one arbitrary percentage.

If displaying a similarity score, explain:

- Which algorithm generated it.
- Which image representations were compared.
- Whether alignment was applied.
- Whether color normalization was applied.
- Whether the score is comparable across sessions.

Clearly distinguish:

- Pixel similarity.
- Structural similarity.
- Perceptual similarity.
- Semantic similarity.

They are not interchangeable.

## 7.2 Difference analysis

Calculate and display:

- Number of changed pixels.
- Percentage of changed pixels.
- Mean absolute error.
- Root mean squared error.
- Maximum difference.
- Difference distribution.
- Color difference.
- Region-level differences.
- Threshold-based changed area.

Support configurable thresholds.

Offer multiple visualizations.

## 7.3 Histograms

Implement histograms for both images.

Support:

- Overall luminance.
- Red channel.
- Green channel.
- Blue channel.
- RGB overlay.
- Alpha where relevant.
- Grayscale.
- Logarithmic scale.
- Linear scale.
- Region-specific histograms.

Display histograms side by side or overlaid.

Allow the user to select a region and update the histogram accordingly.

## 7.4 Exposure analysis

Investigate:

- Underexposure.
- Overexposure.
- Highlight clipping.
- Shadow clipping.
- Dynamic range.
- Luminance distribution.
- Midtone distribution.

Provide visual warnings where justified.

Do not treat every bright or dark image as incorrectly exposed.

## 7.5 Sharpness and focus

Develop meaningful sharpness analysis.

Investigate:

- Laplacian variance.
- Gradient magnitude.
- Edge energy.
- Local contrast.
- Frequency-domain sharpness.
- Region-specific focus estimation.

Identify potentially blurred regions.

Compare focus quality in corresponding regions.

Do not claim that one metric universally determines sharpness.

## 7.6 Noise analysis

Investigate:

- Luminance noise.
- Chrominance noise.
- Noise distribution.
- High-frequency artifacts.
- Denoising artifacts.
- Local noise estimation.

Provide visualizations and numerical measurements.

## 7.7 Color analysis

Support:

- RGB distributions.
- HSV.
- HSL.
- CIELAB.
- CIEDE2000 where appropriate.
- White balance analysis.
- Color temperature estimation.
- Saturation.
- Contrast.
- Dominant colors.
- Color palettes.
- Color distribution comparison.

Allow users to inspect individual colors and compare sampled regions.

Handle different color profiles correctly.

Do not compare raw RGB values across incompatible color spaces and present the result as objective color difference.

## 7.8 Composition analysis

Explore:

- Subject positioning.
- Horizon alignment.
- Rule-of-thirds guides.
- Center-of-interest estimation.
- Cropping differences.
- Perspective.
- Frame balance.
- Edge distribution.

Make these optional visual aids.

Do not pretend subjective artistic quality can be measured perfectly.

## 7.9 Image quality report

Create an optional comparison report.

Possible categories:

- Sharpness.
- Exposure.
- Highlight preservation.
- Shadow preservation.
- Noise.
- Color.
- Contrast.
- Resolution.
- Compression artifacts.
- Structural similarity.
- Alignment quality.

Present individual metrics and explain their limitations.

If recommending a photograph, explain the evidence.

Allow the user to choose which metrics matter for their use case.

# 14. PERFORMANCE — TREAT THIS AS A CORE PRODUCT FEATURE

A photo comparison application can become extremely expensive in memory and GPU usage.

Performance is not something to optimize at the end.

It must influence the architecture from day one.

The application should work well on modest supported Android devices while scaling up on modern hardware.

Do not promise impossible support for devices below the actual Android compatibility baseline.

Establish a realistic minimum supported API level through platform research.

## 14.1 Memory-efficient image loading

Never decode every photograph at full resolution simply because it is visible in a list.

Implement:

- Thumbnail loading.
- Resolution-aware decoding.
- Viewport-based decoding.
- Sampling.
- Lazy loading.
- Image caching.
- Memory budgeting.
- Disk caching where useful.
- Cache eviction.
- Request cancellation.
- Background decoding.
- Efficient bitmap reuse where supported.

For large images, use tiled or region-based rendering.

A user should be able to zoom into a very large image without loading the entire original into GPU memory.

## 14.2 GPU rendering

Investigate:

- Android Canvas.
- Hardware-accelerated rendering.
- OpenGL ES.
- Vulkan where justified.
- Runtime shaders.
- GPU image transformations.

Choose technologies based on measured performance, maintainability, and Android support.

Do not introduce complex GPU infrastructure simply because it sounds advanced.

## 14.3 Processing pipeline

Separate:

1. File access.
2. Metadata extraction.
3. Image decoding.
4. Color conversion.
5. Transformations.
6. Alignment.
7. Analysis.
8. Rendering.
9. Export.

Each stage must have explicit input/output contracts.

Support cancellation.

Do not block the main thread.

## 14.4 Scalability

Test with:

- Two large images.
- Four large images.
- Ten images.
- Hundreds of images in a collection.
- High-resolution images.
- Large RAW files where supported.
- Repeated zooming and panning.
- Rapid comparison-mode switching.
- Background/foreground transitions.

Define measurable memory and frame-time budgets.

Use Android profiling tools to validate them.

# 15. FILE MANAGEMENT AND SAFETY

Implement safe file operations.

Support:

- Android share intents.
- Multiple-image sharing.
- Exporting copies.
- Saving comparison results.
- Moving where permitted.
- Renaming where permitted.
- Deleting with appropriate authorization.
- Recycle/undo behavior where feasible.
- Export location selection.

Deleting images is destructive.

Never make deletion an ambiguous action.

Require a clear confirmation describing:

- Number of files.
- Which files will be deleted.
- Whether originals are affected.
- Whether the operation is reversible.

Use MediaStore and Storage Access Framework appropriately.

Respect scoped storage.

Never silently delete unselected images.

Provide selection previews before bulk deletion.

## 17.3 SOLID, DRY, KISS, YAGNI

Follow these principles:

- Open/closed design.
- Dependency inversion.
- Separation of concerns.
- Avoid duplicated business logic.
- Prefer composition over inheritance.
- Avoid unnecessary abstractions.

- Feature availability.
- Comparison state.
- Workspace configuration.
- Image metadata.
- Selection state.
- User preferences.
- Localization.
- Dependency versions.

Avoid duplicate state.

## 17.5 Dependency injection

Use constructor injection and a suitable dependency injection strategy.

Keep interfaces meaningful.

Avoid abstraction layers that add no actual value.

- Workspace schema versioning.
- Dependency version management.

Use explicit error models.

- Unsupported formats.

- Permission errors.
- Missing files.
- Storage removal.
- Out-of-memory situations.
- Decoder failures.
- Analysis failures.
- Export failures.
- Interrupted operations.

# 25. FINAL INSTRUCTIONS TO THE IMPLEMENTING AGENT

Think deeply before implementing.

Do not merely follow this document mechanically.

Your responsibility is to make the best possible product decisions, including decisions that improve upon the original requirements.

Use first-principles thinking.

For every major feature, ask:

- What is the actual user problem?
- What is the simplest useful solution?
- What happens when the input is imperfect?
- What happens at scale?
- What happens on low-end hardware?
- What happens when users want complete control?
- What happens when an algorithm is uncertain?
- What happens when a future developer needs to extend it?
- What can we learn from other industries?
- What are the second- and third-order consequences of this design?

Think about the complete lifecycle:

```text
Discover images
      |
      v
Understand the collection
      |
      v
Find matching photographs
      |
      v
Choose comparison strategy
      |
      v
Align and normalize
      |
      v
Inspect visually
      |
      v
Analyze measurable differences
      |
      v
Make a decision
      |
      v
Organize, annotate, or rank
      |
      v
Export, share, or continue later
```

Every stage should be independently useful and composable.

Do not force users through a predetermined workflow.

A photographer might only need a 30-second burst-selection experience.

A designer might need pixel-perfect inspection.

A researcher might need repeatable analytical measurements.

A professional investigator might need transparent transformation histories and reproducible comparison settings.

The same application should accommodate these workflows without becoming four different applications.

**Our biggest USP should be that users are not limited to the comparison experience we imagined. We give them an excellent default experience and the underlying tools to construct their own.**

Build for today's Android ecosystem while making architectural decisions that remain understandable and adaptable in 2030 and beyond.

Do not optimize for impressive-looking source code.

Optimize for:

- Correctness.
- Clarity.
- Reliability.
- Performance.
- Extensibility.
- User freedom.
- Maintainability.
- Excellent visual experience.

Start with research and architecture, then implement in verified milestones.

Keep the project runnable throughout development.

Run tests after meaningful changes.

Manually inspect the application.

Measure performance rather than guessing.

Update documentation as decisions and implementations evolve.

If a feature is too ambitious for the current milestone, document its feasibility, interfaces, dependencies, and implementation plan instead of creating a fake implementation.

Do not stop at a beautiful prototype.

**Build a real, polished, deeply useful, production-quality native Android application.**

Think beyond the obvious. Question the defaults. Make the difficult things understandable. Make the simple things effortless.

The goal is not to make another image comparator.

**The goal is to build the visual comparison workspace that people wish already existed.**
